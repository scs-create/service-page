## 1. Understanding the Master Service Agreement

A Master Service Agreement (MSA) establishes the general legal and commercial terms governing an ongoing relationship between a service provider and a client. It provides a consistent contractual framework for multiple projects or engagements, reducing the need to renegotiate standard terms for every assignment.

Specific services, deliverables, fees, and timelines are typically documented in Statements of Work (SOWs), service orders, or project schedules.

An MSA commonly addresses:

- Scope of services and contractual obligations.
- Fees, payment terms, and expenses.
- Confidentiality and data protection.
- Intellectual property ownership.
- Liability and indemnification.
- Change management and acceptance procedures.
- Termination and dispute resolution.

## 2. Define the Scope of Services

Ambiguous service descriptions create uncertainty about contractual obligations and frequently lead to scope creep—the gradual expansion of work beyond the original agreement without corresponding adjustments to fees or timelines.

Each engagement should specify:

- Services included in the agreed fee.
- Services expressly excluded.
- Expected deliverables and applicable standards.
- Project duration and agreed milestones.
- Conditions requiring a separate engagement.

**Example:** Rather than describing an engagement simply as business support, specify the particular administrative services, regulatory filing coordination, and compliance reporting covered by the agreement.

Clear boundaries establish a common understanding of the engagement and reduce disputes over additional work.

## 3. Specify Deliverables and Acceptance Criteria

A service description should identify measurable outputs rather than relying solely on general statements about activities.

Depending on the engagement, deliverables may include reports, policies, contracts, compliance registers, operational manuals, or implementation documents.

For each deliverable, establish:

- Required content and format.
- Applicable quality or performance standards.
- Delivery dates and dependencies.
- The person responsible for reviewing it.
- The procedure and deadline for acceptance or rejection.

A defined review period and a written acceptance procedure help prevent indefinite revisions and disagreements over whether contractual obligations have been fulfilled.

## 4. Establish a Formal Change-Control Procedure

Changes in business requirements are normal, but additional work should not automatically become part of the original fee.

A written change-control procedure should require:

1. **Request:** The client describes the proposed change.
1. **Assessment:** The parties evaluate its effect on scope, resources, deliverables, and deadlines.
1. **Pricing:** Any additional fees or expenses are determined.
1. **Approval:** The parties approve the revised terms in writing.
1. **Implementation:** Work on the change begins in accordance with the approved arrangement.

The agreement should identify who can authorise changes and how approved variations affect existing obligations.

## 5. Allocate Responsibilities and Set Timelines

Contractual responsibilities should be assigned to prevent delays arising from unclear ownership of tasks or decisions.

The agreement should identify the parties' respective obligations concerning:

- Provision of accurate information and supporting documents.
- Access to relevant systems, personnel, and facilities.
- Reviews, approvals, and feedback.
- Project coordination and communication.
- Compliance with applicable deadlines.

Timelines should account for dependencies, including client approvals and third-party or regulatory processes. Where these dependencies affect delivery, the agreement should specify how delays will be addressed and whether deadlines may be extended.

## 6. Protect Fees and Payment Rights

Payment provisions should reflect the commercial structure of the engagement and establish clear consequences for non-payment.

Relevant terms include:

- Fees, invoicing arrangements, and payment deadlines.
- Advance payments or milestone-based payments.
- Treatment of taxes and approved expenses.
- Interest on overdue amounts, where lawful and agreed.
- Suspension of services for material payment defaults.
- Outstanding payment obligations upon termination.

Any suspension or termination right should be subject to the contract's terms and applicable law.

## 7. Set Revision Limits and Approval Procedures

Unlimited revisions can increase delivery costs and delay project completion.

The agreement or relevant SOW should specify the number of included revision rounds, the process for submitting consolidated feedback, and the charges applicable to additional revisions.

It should also distinguish between corrections required because a deliverable fails to meet the agreed specifications and changes arising from new preferences or requirements.

This distinction supports fair treatment of both parties and provides a clear basis for determining when additional fees are appropriate.

## 8. Address Confidentiality, Data Protection, and Intellectual Property

### Confidentiality

Identify the information protected, permitted uses, authorised disclosures, applicable exceptions, and the duration of confidentiality obligations.

### Data Protection

Where personal data is involved, establish the parties' respective responsibilities for lawful processing, security, access, retention, disclosure, and deletion or return of data, as applicable.

Contractual provisions should reflect the requirements of the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 and any other applicable data protection laws.

### Intellectual Property

Distinguish between:

- **Pre-existing intellectual property:** Materials, tools, templates, methodologies, and other rights owned or licensed before the engagement.
- **Project deliverables:** Materials created specifically under the engagement.
- **Third-party materials:** Content or software subject to separate ownership or licensing terms.

The agreement should establish ownership or licensing rights, permitted uses, and any conditions governing transfer. Where ownership is intended to transfer, the applicable terms and formalities should be expressly addressed.

## 9. Allocate Liability and Manage Risk

Liability provisions determine how contractual risks are allocated when a party suffers loss arising from an engagement.

Relevant considerations include:

- The types of loss covered or excluded.
- Any agreed limitation of liability.
- Exceptions to liability limitations.
- Indemnification obligations.
- Insurance requirements, where appropriate.

A liability cap should be commercially reasonable and carefully drafted. Its operation may depend on the wording of the agreement and applicable law; no single formula is suitable for every engagement.

## 10. Establish Termination and Dispute Resolution Procedures

The agreement should define the circumstances in which either party may terminate the relationship and the procedure for doing so.

Relevant provisions include:

- Termination for convenience, where agreed.
- Termination for material breach.
- Notice and opportunities to remedy breaches.
- Immediate termination in specified circumstances.
- Payment for completed work and approved commitments.
- Return of property, transfer of deliverables, and treatment of confidential information.
- Survival of obligations intended to continue after termination.

A dispute-resolution clause should identify the agreed process, which may include negotiation, mediation, arbitration, or court proceedings, subject to applicable law.

## 11. Maintain Effective Contract Management Records

A signed agreement is only effective as a management tool when its obligations and deadlines are monitored.

A contract management register should record:

- Contract reference and parties.
- Scope, value, and commencement date.
- Key deliverables and milestones.
- Payment obligations and outstanding invoices.
- Renewal dates and termination notice periods.
- Approved variations and outstanding obligations.

Regular reviews help identify approaching deadlines, unapproved changes, payment risks, and obligations requiring attention.

## 12. Common Contract Drafting Errors

The following practices increase commercial and legal risk:

- Commencing work without a properly approved agreement.
- Using vague service descriptions.
- Failing to identify exclusions and additional charges.
- Accepting material changes without written approval.
- Leaving revision limits undefined.
- Omitting client responsibilities and delivery dependencies.
- Failing to document approvals and variations.
- Leaving intellectual property rights uncertain.
- Overlooking termination, liability, or dispute-resolution provisions.
- Failing to monitor contractual obligations after execution.

## Conclusion

An effective Master Service Agreement establishes clear expectations, defines the limits of an engagement, and provides a structured process for managing changes. Its value lies not only in allocating legal rights and obligations but also in supporting sound commercial decisions throughout the service relationship.

Careful drafting, appropriate supporting documents, written approvals, and consistent contract management reduce avoidable disputes and help protect the interests of all parties.
