Many business owners assume that if their accountant maintains accurate records, prepares financial statements and handles tax filings, they have a complete picture of their company's financial health.

That assumption can be costly.

A business may have properly maintained accounting records and still struggle with cash flow, overdue customer payments, unprofitable contracts and rising operating expenses. Its financial statements may be accurate, yet management may lack the information needed to identify problems early and make timely decisions.

Consider a company whose annual sales reach ₦100 million. Its accountant confirms that the books are up to date and the relevant financial reports have been prepared. However, management subsequently discovers that ₦45 million remains unpaid by customers, several projects are operating below their expected margins, and significant supplier payments are approaching.

The accountant may have done nothing wrong. The problem is that accurate accounting records do not automatically provide the operational visibility management needs.

This is where **commercial tracking** becomes important.

Statutory accounting supports legal compliance and financial reporting. Commercial tracking helps management monitor transactions, commitments, collections and business performance as events unfold. Although the two disciplines overlap, they serve different purposes.

Understanding the distinction is essential for businesses that want to protect their cash flow, improve profitability and make informed decisions.

## 1. What Is Statutory Accounting?

Statutory accounting involves maintaining and preparing financial records and reports in accordance with applicable laws, regulations and financial reporting requirements.

For Nigerian businesses, these obligations may arise under company law, tax legislation, applicable financial reporting standards and sector-specific regulations.

Depending on the nature, size and regulatory status of a business, statutory accounting and related financial compliance activities may include:

- Preparing annual financial statements.
- Maintaining appropriate accounting records.
- Recording revenue, expenses, assets and liabilities.
- Supporting statutory audits where required.
- Preparing tax computations and relevant tax returns.
- Maintaining documentation for tax assessments and regulatory reviews.
- Providing financial information required by investors, lenders or regulators.

Typical financial statements include:

**Statement of profit or loss:** Reports income and expenses over a period and shows whether the business generated an accounting profit or loss.

**Statement of financial position:** Presents the company's assets, liabilities and equity at a particular date.

**Statement of cash flows:** Shows how cash and cash equivalents have moved through operating, investing and financing activities, subject to the applicable reporting requirements.

These reports help stakeholders understand the financial position and performance of a business.

However, financial statements are generally prepared for defined reporting periods. They may not, by themselves, provide the detailed operational information required to manage individual invoices, project margins, customer collection activities and upcoming payment commitments every day.

**The primary question statutory accounting helps answer is: Are the business's financial records and reports properly maintained and prepared for the applicable reporting and compliance requirements?**

## 2. What Is Commercial Tracking?

Commercial tracking is the systematic monitoring of the financial and operational activities that affect a business's revenue, costs, contractual commitments, collections and profitability.

It converts transaction-level information into practical management information.

Rather than waiting for a monthly or annual report, management can use commercial tracking to identify what requires attention now.

Typical commercial tracking activities include:

- Monitoring invoices issued and payments received.
- Identifying overdue customer balances.
- Tracking supplier invoices and payment deadlines.
- Monitoring contract values, milestones and outstanding balances.
- Comparing project revenue against direct costs.
- Reviewing revenue by customer, product, service or business unit.
- Monitoring sales performance against targets.
- Forecasting expected cash inflows and outflows.
- Identifying contracts with declining margins.
- Following up on outstanding commercial commitments.

For example, an accounting system may record that a customer owes the business ₦4.5 million. A commercial tracking system adds operational detail: when the invoice became due, how long it has remained unpaid, who is responsible for following up, what the customer has communicated and when the next action should occur.

This additional information helps management decide what to do.

**The primary question commercial tracking helps answer is: What is happening in the business commercially, what requires attention, and what action should management take?**

## 3. Commercial Tracking, Management Accounting and Bookkeeping: What Is the Difference?

These terms are related, but they are not interchangeable.

| Discipline | Primary purpose | Typical activities |
|---|---|---|
| Bookkeeping | Record financial transactions accurately and systematically. | Recording invoices, receipts, payments, expenses and journal entries. |
| Statutory accounting and financial reporting | Prepare financial information in accordance with applicable reporting and legal requirements. | Preparing financial statements, maintaining supporting records and meeting relevant reporting obligations. |
| Management accounting | Analyse financial information to support planning, cost control and management decisions. | Budgeting, variance analysis, cost allocation, forecasting and profitability analysis. |
| Commercial tracking | Monitor ongoing commercial activities, commitments and performance to support timely operational decisions. | Tracking collections, contract milestones, customer balances, project performance and payment commitments. |

In practice, these functions often overlap.

For example, a project profitability report may be part of management accounting, while the underlying project tracker may be maintained by a commercial or operations team.

The important distinction is not the department responsible for the work. It is whether the business can move from recording what happened to understanding what it means and deciding what to do next.

## 4. Why Accurate Accounting Records Do Not Guarantee Financial Control

A company can maintain accurate books and still encounter serious commercial problems.

This happens because financial accuracy and operational visibility are different things.

Consider four common situations.

### A. Revenue has been recorded, but the cash has not arrived

A business completes a service and issues an invoice for ₦8 million.

The revenue may be recognised in the appropriate accounting period, depending on the applicable accounting rules and the terms of the transaction. However, the customer may not pay for another 60 or 90 days.

The accounting records can be correct while the business struggles to pay salaries, suppliers or other operating expenses.

Commercial tracking helps management monitor the invoice, assess collection risks and plan around the expected receipt.

### B. The business is profitable overall, but individual projects are losing money

A company may report an overall accounting profit while several contracts are consuming more resources than anticipated.

Without project-level tracking, management may not discover the problem until the work is complete.

Tracking revenue, direct costs, labour, subcontractor expenses and other relevant project costs helps management identify underperforming engagements before the losses increase.

### C. Sales are increasing, but margins are declining

Higher sales do not necessarily mean a healthier business.

If the cost of delivering products or services increases faster than revenue, gross margins may decline even as sales volumes rise.

Commercial and management reports help management distinguish revenue growth from profitable growth.

### D. Supplier obligations are approaching faster than customer receipts

A business may have substantial outstanding customer invoices but insufficient cash to meet its immediate obligations.

Without a forward-looking view of expected receipts and payments, management may commit to expenses it cannot comfortably fund.

A commercial cash-flow forecast helps identify potential shortfalls before they become emergencies.

These examples illustrate why businesses need more than a record of completed transactions. They also need a structured view of outstanding obligations, expected receipts and emerging performance risks.

## 5. Create a Customer Invoice Register

An invoice register provides a central record of invoices issued to customers.

At a minimum, it should contain:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Invoice number | Provides a unique reference for each invoice. |
| Customer name | Identifies the party responsible for payment. |
| Invoice date | Shows when the invoice was issued. |
| Invoice amount | Records the original amount billed. |
| Due date | Identifies when payment is expected under the agreed terms. |
| Amount received | Shows payments received against the invoice. |
| Outstanding balance | Shows the amount remaining unpaid. |
| Status | Indicates whether the invoice is unpaid, partially paid, paid, disputed or otherwise unresolved. |
| Responsible officer | Identifies who monitors the invoice and follows up. |
| Next action date | Establishes when the next collection action is due. |

For example:

| Invoice No. | Customer | Invoice Amount | Due Date | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| INV-001 | ABC Ltd | ₦500,000 | 15 November | Outstanding |
| INV-002 | XYZ Ltd | ₦750,000 | 20 November | Paid |
| INV-003 | LMN Ltd | ₦1,200,000 | 10 November | Overdue |

The dates above are illustrative.

The register should be updated whenever an invoice is issued, amended, paid, disputed or otherwise resolved. Where partial payments occur, the outstanding balance should be recalculated and reconciled against the accounting records.

**Practical rule:** Every invoice should have a clear status, an accountable person and a next action where necessary.

## 6. Develop an Accounts Receivable Tracker

An invoice register records invoices. An accounts receivable tracker adds a collection-management perspective.

It helps the business identify which customers owe money, how long the balances have remained outstanding and what action is needed.

A useful tracker should include:

- Customer name and contact details.
- Invoice references and outstanding balances.
- Contractual payment terms.
- Invoice due dates.
- Number of days past due.
- Collection history.
- Disputed amounts and reasons for the dispute.
- Previous follow-up dates.
- Agreed payment arrangements.
- Responsible collection officer.
- Next follow-up date.
- Escalation status.

### Use an ageing analysis

Receivables ageing groups outstanding balances according to how long they have remained unpaid.

A typical structure is:

| Ageing category | Meaning | Suggested management response |
|---|---|---|
| Not yet due | Payment deadline has not passed. | Confirm invoice receipt and monitor the due date. |
| 1 to 30 days overdue | Payment is slightly late. | Send a reminder and confirm the reason for delay. |
| 31 to 60 days overdue | Collection risk is increasing. | Escalate follow-up and obtain a clear payment commitment. |
| 61 to 90 days overdue | The balance requires closer attention. | Review the customer's payment history and consider formal escalation. |
| More than 90 days overdue | The balance may present significant collection risk. | Assess recovery options, contractual remedies and appropriate legal advice. |

These categories are illustrative and should be adapted to the business's payment terms, industry and customer risk profile.

For example:

| Customer | Outstanding Balance | Days Overdue | Next Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Client A | ₦1,200,000 | 15 | Send reminder |
| Client B | ₦4,500,000 | 90 | Escalate collection |
| Client C | ₦850,000 | 0 | Monitor due date |

The purpose is not merely to identify debtors. It is to establish a disciplined process for collecting money that the business has earned.

## 7. Implement Supplier Payment Tracking

Businesses must monitor outgoing obligations as carefully as incoming revenue.

A supplier payment tracker helps management understand what the company owes, when payment is due and whether sufficient funds are expected to be available.

It should record:

- Supplier name.
- Invoice reference.
- Description of goods or services.
- Invoice amount.
- Approved amount payable.
- Payment terms.
- Due date.
- Amount already paid.
- Outstanding balance.
- Approval status.
- Disputed amounts.
- Planned payment date.
- Responsible officer.

A weekly review helps management identify upcoming obligations and prioritise payments.

For example, a supplier may have issued an invoice for ₦3 million, payable within 30 days. If the invoice is not entered into the payment tracker, management may overlook it while allocating available cash to other expenses.

The consequences can include late-payment charges, supply interruptions, damaged commercial relationships or contractual disputes.

However, payment tracking should not be confused with automatic payment approval. A payment must still undergo the business's required verification, authorisation and fraud-prevention controls.

**The objective is to ensure that obligations are visible, verified and planned for, not simply to pay every invoice as soon as it appears.**

## 8. Track Individual Project Profitability

Revenue alone does not reveal whether a project is commercially successful.

A business should monitor each significant project or engagement against its expected financial performance.

A project tracker may include:

- Project or contract reference.
- Customer.
- Agreed contract value.
- Revenue earned or recognised, as applicable.
- Amount invoiced.
- Amount collected.
- Estimated total delivery cost.
- Actual costs incurred.
- Remaining cost to complete.
- Expected gross profit.
- Expected gross margin.
- Project completion status.
- Variances from budget.
- Outstanding commercial issues.

### Example: Two projects with different margins

Suppose a consultancy delivers two projects.

| Item | Project A | Project B |
|---|---|---|
| Contract value | ₦5,000,000 | ₦5,000,000 |
| Direct delivery costs | ₦2,500,000 | ₦4,200,000 |
| Gross profit before other expenses | ₦2,500,000 | ₦800,000 |
| Gross margin | 50% | 16% |

Both projects generate the same revenue, but Project A contributes substantially more before overheads and other expenses.

If management monitors only total sales, this difference may remain hidden.

Project-level tracking can help management identify underpriced contracts, excessive delivery costs, scope changes and operational inefficiencies.

It can also support better decisions about future pricing and which types of engagements the business should pursue.

The calculations must use consistent cost definitions. Gross profit at project level is not necessarily the same as the business's final accounting profit.

## 9. Create a Contract Revenue and Obligation Dashboard

For contract-based businesses, a contract register should track both commercial value and contractual obligations.

This is particularly important for consultancies, construction companies, professional service firms, technology providers and businesses that deliver work in stages.

A contract dashboard should capture:

- Contract reference and customer.
- Contract start and end dates.
- Total contract value.
- Scope of work.
- Delivery milestones.
- Invoicing milestones.
- Payment terms.
- Amount invoiced.
- Amount collected.
- Outstanding customer balance.
- Costs incurred.
- Outstanding deliverables.
- Variation orders and approved scope changes.
- Renewal or termination dates.
- Outstanding contractual risks.

Consider the following example:

| Contract | Contract Value | Amount Collected | Outstanding Contract Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contract A | ₦5,000,000 | ₦3,000,000 | ₦2,000,000 |
| Contract B | ₦10,000,000 | ₦10,000,000 | ₦0 |

The final column is calculated by subtracting the amount collected from the total contract value. It represents the value not yet collected, not necessarily the amount currently invoiced or legally due.

These figures can differ where work has not yet been performed, milestones have not been reached, invoices have not been issued or amounts are disputed.

A reliable dashboard should distinguish among:

1. **Contract value:** The total agreed commercial value, subject to the contract's terms.
1. **Value of work performed:** The value attributable to work completed or progress achieved.
1. **Amount invoiced:** The amount billed to the customer.
1. **Amount due:** The amount payable under the applicable payment terms.
1. **Amount collected:** The amount actually received.
1. **Remaining contract value:** The portion not yet earned, billed or collected, depending on the metric being reported.

These distinctions prevent management from treating the entire value of an uncompleted contract as immediately available cash.

## 10. Monitor Cash Flow Weekly

A company can be profitable and still run out of cash.

This is because accounting profit and available cash are not the same. Revenue may be recognised before payment is received, while expenses and contractual obligations may require cash payments before related customer receipts arrive.

For this reason, businesses should maintain a forward-looking cash-flow forecast.

At a minimum, review:

**Opening cash balance:** Cash available at the beginning of the forecast period.

**Expected inflows:** Customer payments and other receipts expected during the period.

**Expected outflows:** Salaries, supplier payments, rent, taxes, debt repayments and other planned disbursements.

**Closing cash balance:** The projected cash available after expected inflows and outflows.

A simplified forecast is:

Closing cash balance = Opening cash balance + Expected cash inflows − Expected cash outflows.

### Example

A business begins the week with ₦6 million in available cash.

It expects to collect ₦4 million and pay ₦7.5 million in operating obligations.

Its projected closing cash balance is:

₦6 million + ₦4 million − ₦7.5 million = ₦2.5 million.

If a major customer delays the expected ₦4 million payment, the projected closing balance falls to ₦2.5 million less ₦4 million, or a negative ₦1.5 million, assuming all other cash flows remain unchanged.

This identifies a potential funding gap before the payments fall due.

For businesses with volatile receipts or significant obligations, a rolling 13-week cash-flow forecast can provide a useful planning horizon. Smaller businesses may begin with a weekly forecast covering the next four to eight weeks and extend it as their needs develop.

The forecast should be updated using actual receipts and payments, not merely copied forward from previous weeks.

## 11. Track Revenue Performance Beyond Total Sales

A revenue figure tells management how much business was generated. It does not necessarily explain where the revenue came from, what drove the result or whether it is sustainable.

Commercial reporting should break revenue down into useful categories, such as:

- Product or service line.
- Customer or customer segment.
- Salesperson or account manager.
- Branch or location.
- Sales channel.
- Contract type.
- New business versus repeat business.
- Actual performance versus target.

For example, a consultancy may discover that its tax advisory services generate 35% of revenue but a significantly higher gross margin than another service line. Alternatively, it may find that a major customer contributes a large proportion of sales, creating concentration risk.

Such findings can influence pricing, marketing expenditure, staffing and business development priorities.

When analysing revenue, management should distinguish between invoiced amounts, cash collected and revenue recognised under the applicable accounting framework. These figures serve different purposes and should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 12. Monitor Expenses and Cost Trends

Businesses sometimes focus on increasing sales without examining how much it costs to generate and deliver that revenue.

Commercial tracking should therefore include a structured view of expenditure.

Common categories include:

- Salaries and employee-related costs.
- Rent and facilities.
- Marketing and advertising.
- Technology and software.
- Professional fees.
- Utilities and communications.
- Transport and logistics.
- Subcontractor expenses.
- Financing costs.
- Other operating expenses.

Management should compare actual expenditure against approved budgets and investigate material variances.

For example, if monthly marketing expenditure rises from ₦800,000 to ₦1.5 million without a corresponding improvement in qualified leads or sales, management should investigate the cause.

The increase may be justified, but the decision should be supported by evidence.

Where appropriate, analyse costs by project, department, service line or customer. This makes it easier to identify which activities are consuming resources and whether those costs are commercially justified.

## 13. Build a Management Dashboard That Supports Decisions

A dashboard should not be a collection of attractive charts. It should help management identify material developments and determine what action is required.

An effective commercial dashboard may include the following indicators:

| Indicator | What It Reveals | Suggested Review Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue against target | Whether sales performance is on track. | Weekly or monthly |
| Gross margin | Whether revenue is generating an adequate contribution before other expenses. | Monthly |
| Overdue receivables | The amount and age of unpaid customer balances. | Weekly |
| Receivables collection rate | How effectively the business converts billed amounts into cash over a defined period. | Monthly |
| Upcoming payables | Payments that may affect short-term liquidity. | Weekly |
| Forecast closing cash balance | Whether the business may face a cash shortfall. | Weekly |
| Project margin variance | Whether individual projects are performing against budget. | Weekly or monthly |
| Customer concentration | The extent to which revenue depends on a small number of customers. | Monthly or quarterly |
| Budget variance | Whether actual income or expenditure differs materially from plan. | Monthly |
| Contract milestone status | Whether delivery, invoicing and payment milestones are on schedule. | Weekly |

Not every business needs every indicator. A small professional services firm may initially need only an invoice register, receivables ageing report, supplier payment schedule, project profitability tracker and rolling cash-flow forecast.

The dashboard should grow with the business.

Each indicator should have a clear definition, a responsible owner and a threshold that triggers investigation or action.

## 14. Establish Monthly Commercial Performance Reviews

Tracking becomes valuable when management regularly reviews the information and acts on it.

A monthly commercial review should bring together finance, operations, sales and other relevant teams.

The agenda may cover:

**Revenue performance:** What did the business generate compared with its target?

**Collections:** Which invoices remain unpaid, and which require escalation?

**Expenditure:** Which costs exceeded the budget, and why?

**Project profitability:** Which engagements are performing below expectations?

**Contract performance:** Are delivery and payment milestones being met?

**Cash flow:** Will expected receipts cover upcoming obligations?

**Customer risk:** Are there customers with deteriorating payment behaviour or excessive balances?

**Commercial opportunities:** Are there opportunities to improve pricing, renew contracts or increase profitable sales?

**Management actions:** What decisions must be made, who is responsible and when must each action be completed?

A review meeting should conclude with an action register rather than simply a discussion of figures.

For instance, if a customer owes ₦6 million and has repeatedly missed payment commitments, the meeting should establish the next collection action, the responsible officer, the deadline and the point at which the matter should be escalated.

Without assigned responsibilities and follow-up dates, reporting can become a passive exercise.

## 15. Integrate Commercial Tracking with the Accounting System

Commercial tracking should complement accounting rather than create a competing set of financial records.

A common mistake is to maintain separate spreadsheets across finance, sales and operations without reconciling them. Over time, the figures may diverge because of duplicate entries, missed payments, inconsistent definitions or outdated information.

A better approach is to establish clear relationships between the systems.

| Accounting Function | Commercial Tracking Function |
|---|---|
| Records invoices issued. | Monitors due dates, collection progress and overdue balances. |
| Records customer receipts. | Updates collection status and identifies unresolved balances. |
| Records supplier invoices and payments. | Tracks upcoming obligations and planned payment dates. |
| Records revenue and expenses. | Analyses performance by customer, project, product or service. |
| Produces financial statements. | Provides operational reports, forecasts and management alerts. |
| Maintains transaction records. | Assigns follow-up actions and monitors commercial commitments. |

Where the business uses accounting software, a CRM, a contract management platform or an enterprise resource planning system, determine which platform is the authoritative source for each type of information.

For example:

- The accounting system may be the authoritative source for posted invoices, receipts and balances.
- The CRM may manage sales opportunities and customer interactions.
- The contract register may monitor contractual obligations and milestones.
- A management dashboard may consolidate approved information from these sources.

Where full integration is not immediately affordable, a controlled spreadsheet-based system can be sufficient for a small business.

However, it should have a designated owner, standardised fields, appropriate access permissions, version control and a regular reconciliation process.

The goal is not to purchase the most sophisticated software. It is to establish a reliable system that management can trust.

## 16. Establish Controls to Keep Commercial Data Reliable

A tracking system is only as useful as the information it contains.

Poorly maintained trackers can create false confidence and lead to bad decisions.

Businesses should therefore establish basic controls.

### Assign ownership

Identify the person responsible for maintaining each register and the person responsible for reviewing it.

### Standardise definitions

Ensure that terms such as revenue, amount due, amount collected, outstanding contract value and project cost have consistent meanings across reports.

### Reconcile records

Compare commercial trackers with the accounting system and supporting documents at defined intervals. Investigate differences instead of adjusting figures without explanation.

### Control access

Restrict access to sensitive financial and customer information according to employees' responsibilities.

### Preserve an audit trail

Record material changes, approvals and adjustments so that the business can determine who changed information and why.

### Review exceptions

Investigate overdue invoices, unapproved expenses, unusual payment requests, missing documentation and significant budget variances.

### Protect against fraud

Verify changes to supplier bank details through an established independent process. Separate payment initiation and approval where practicable, and avoid relying solely on email instructions for sensitive payment changes.

These controls help ensure that commercial tracking improves financial discipline rather than becoming another source of unreliable information.

## 17. Practical Implementation Plan for a Growing Business

A business does not need to implement every tracker at once.

The following phased approach provides a manageable starting point.

### Phase 1: Establish visibility

**Priority: Outstanding money and immediate obligations.**

Create:

- Customer invoice register.
- Accounts receivable ageing report.
- Supplier payment schedule.
- Current cash position report.

At this stage, management should be able to identify who owes the business money, what the business owes others and whether upcoming obligations can be met.

### Phase 2: Understand profitability

**Priority: Where money is being generated and consumed.**

Introduce:

- Project profitability tracker.
- Revenue analysis by product or service.
- Expense analysis by category.
- Budget-versus-actual report.

This helps management identify unprofitable activities, excessive costs and opportunities to improve pricing.

### Phase 3: Monitor commitments

**Priority: Contract performance and future obligations.**

Develop:

- Contract register.
- Delivery and payment milestone tracker.
- Renewal and expiry calendar.
- Commercial risk register.

This is especially important for businesses that depend on long-term contracts or milestone-based payments.

### Phase 4: Forecast and improve

**Priority: Forward-looking decisions.**

Implement:

- Rolling cash-flow forecast.
- Revenue and collection forecasts.
- Management dashboard.
- Monthly commercial review meetings.
- Action register with assigned responsibilities and deadlines.

At this stage, the business moves from simply recording what has happened to anticipating risks and managing performance.

### Phase 5: Automate where justified

Once the process is stable, management can assess whether accounting software, CRM tools, workflow automation or integrated reporting systems would reduce manual work and improve reliability.

Automation should follow a well-defined process. Automating inconsistent or poorly controlled records can simply make errors occur faster.

## 18. Practical Example: A Consultancy That Appeared Profitable but Was Losing Control

Consider a growing compliance consultancy that relies on accounting reports to assess its performance.

The company has secured several contracts, increased its annual revenue and maintained its accounting records.

Management initially believes that the business is performing well.

A commercial review, however, reveals the following:

- ₦12 million in overdue customer invoices.
- Two major projects with substantially lower margins than anticipated.
- Several contracts approaching delivery deadlines without adequate cost forecasts.
- Supplier obligations falling due before expected customer payments.
- No central record of who is responsible for following up on outstanding invoices.

The accounting records may be accurate, but the company lacks sufficient commercial visibility.

### Actions taken

The company introduces:

1. A central invoice register.
1. A receivables ageing report with assigned collection responsibilities.
1. A contract dashboard showing delivery and payment milestones.
1. Project profitability reports.
1. A weekly cash-flow forecast.
1. A monthly commercial review with documented action points.

### Expected benefits

These measures can help the business:

- Identify collection problems earlier.
- Prioritise overdue customer balances.
- Recognise unprofitable engagements before committing further resources.
- Plan supplier payments more effectively.
- Improve future contract pricing.
- Assign accountability for commercial follow-up.
- Make decisions using more current and relevant information.

The improvements are not automatic. They depend on reliable data, disciplined follow-up and management's willingness to act on the findings.

Nevertheless, the underlying lesson is clear: **accurate accounting tells a business what its records show; effective commercial tracking helps management act on the financial and operational implications.**

## 19. Common Commercial Tracking Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Assuming accounting reports provide every management answer

Financial statements are essential, but they may not contain the transaction-level detail needed for daily commercial decisions.

**Better approach:** Supplement financial reporting with operational trackers and management reports.

### Mistake 2: Tracking invoices without tracking collection actions

A register that merely lists overdue balances does not guarantee that anyone will pursue them.

**Better approach:** Assign a responsible person, next action and follow-up date to every material outstanding balance.

### Mistake 3: Confusing contract value with cash available

A signed contract does not mean the entire contract value has been earned, invoiced or collected.

**Better approach:** Separate contract value, revenue earned, amounts invoiced, amounts due and cash received.

### Mistake 4: Monitoring total revenue without analysing margins

Higher sales may conceal rising delivery costs or underpriced contracts.

**Better approach:** Monitor project, product or service profitability using consistent cost definitions.

### Mistake 5: Ignoring supplier obligations

A business may focus on customer collections while overlooking payments that could disrupt operations.

**Better approach:** Maintain a verified payment schedule and review upcoming obligations against forecast cash availability.

### Mistake 6: Reviewing cash flow only at month-end

Monthly reporting may reveal a cash shortfall after the business has already committed to payments.

**Better approach:** Maintain a rolling forecast and update it as actual receipts, payments and expected dates change.

### Mistake 7: Maintaining disconnected spreadsheets

Different teams may report different balances for the same customer or contract.

**Better approach:** Establish authoritative records, standard definitions and routine reconciliations.

### Mistake 8: Building dashboards that do not lead to decisions

A dashboard filled with figures is not necessarily a useful management tool.

**Better approach:** Define thresholds, responsibilities and escalation procedures for significant exceptions.

### Mistake 9: Treating forecasts as guaranteed outcomes

Expected customer payments can be delayed, and unplanned expenses can arise.

**Better approach:** Update forecasts regularly and test alternative scenarios where the business is exposed to significant uncertainty.

### Mistake 10: Failing to connect commercial and financial teams

Sales, operations and finance may each hold important information without sharing it consistently.

**Better approach:** Establish a common reporting process and regular cross-functional reviews.

A business that answers no to several of these questions may have gaps in its commercial control environment. It should prioritise the gaps that create the greatest immediate risks to cash flow, profitability and contractual performance.

## Conclusion

Statutory accounting and commercial tracking are complementary disciplines, but they are not substitutes for one another.

Statutory accounting supports financial reporting and applicable legal and regulatory obligations. Commercial tracking helps management monitor collections, costs, contracts, commitments and business performance as they develop.

A business can maintain accurate financial statements and still experience cash-flow pressure, overdue receivables, underperforming projects and declining margins if it does not actively monitor its commercial activities.

The answer is not to abandon accounting records or duplicate them in endless spreadsheets. It is to connect reliable financial records with practical management information, clear responsibilities and timely action.

For a small business, this may begin with an invoice register, a supplier payment schedule, a project tracker and a weekly cash-flow forecast. As the business grows, these tools can develop into integrated reporting systems and structured commercial review processes.

Ultimately, **financial records explain the business's financial position and performance, while commercial tracking helps management understand emerging issues, protect cash flow and make better-informed decisions.**

Businesses that use both are better equipped to identify problems early, manage their obligations and pursue growth without losing sight of financial control.
