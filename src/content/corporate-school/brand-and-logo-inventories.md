Businesses create valuable intellectual assets through their brands, logos, marketing materials, software, original content and internal processes. Yet many organisations lack a reliable record of what they own, who created it, where it is stored or whether the necessary rights have been secured.

These gaps can create problems during staff transitions, commercial transactions, investor due diligence and intellectual property disputes.

A brand and intellectual property (IP) inventory provides a structured record of these assets, their ownership, protection status and commercial use. It helps businesses identify what requires protection, resolve ownership uncertainties and manage their intellectual property more effectively.

## 1. Identify Your Intellectual Assets

Intellectual property extends beyond registered trademarks and patents. Begin by identifying the intellectual assets created, acquired, licensed or used by the business.

Common categories include:

- **Brand assets:** Business names, product names, logos, slogans and distinctive visual elements.
- **Creative assets:** Photographs, videos, advertisements, website content, brochures and training materials.
- **Business materials:** Standard operating procedures, internal manuals, templates and proprietary documentation.
- **Technology assets:** Software, source code, applications, website designs and custom digital systems.
- **Innovations:** Inventions, industrial designs, technical processes and confidential know-how.
- **Digital assets:** Domain names, digital publications and other business-related content or resources.

Not every item automatically qualifies for intellectual property protection. Classification depends on the nature of the asset, applicable law and the rights available.

## 2. Conduct a Business-Wide Asset Review

Identify assets across departments rather than relying solely on the marketing or legal team.

Relevant personnel may include management, marketing, IT, operations, HR, product development and external contractors.

Ask each department to identify:

- Materials created for the business.
- Brands and product names currently in use.
- Software and systems developed or commissioned.
- Content purchased, licensed or supplied by third parties.
- Confidential processes and commercially valuable information.
- Existing registrations, licences and ownership agreements.

Collect available supporting documents alongside the asset descriptions. The objective is to build a sufficiently complete inventory before assessing protection priorities.

## 3. Create a Central Intellectual Property Register

Record each identified asset in a central register that can be maintained as the business evolves.

A practical register may contain the following fields:

| Field | Information to record |
|---|---|
| Asset ID | Unique reference number |
| Asset name | Name or description |
| Category | Trademark, copyright, software, design or other category |
| Creator or source | Employee, contractor, agency or third party |
| Rights holder | Person or entity believed to own the rights |
| Creation or acquisition date | Relevant date and supporting evidence |
| Protection status | Registered, unregistered, pending, licensed or under review |
| Supporting documents | Contracts, registration certificates and related records |
| Storage location | Link to the approved repository |
| Responsible person | Person overseeing the asset |
| Next action | Registration, ownership verification, renewal or review |

Distinguish confirmed ownership from information that remains unverified. An inventory records and organises the available information; it does not, by itself, establish legal ownership.

## 4. Catalogue Business and Product Names

Business names, product names and service brands may carry substantial commercial value.

For each name, record:

- The exact name and relevant variations.
- The products or services associated with it.
- The date and evidence of first use, where available.
- The markets and jurisdictions in which it is used.
- Relevant company-name or trademark registrations.
- Pending applications, objections or disputes.
- Existing licences or agreements governing its use.

Company-name registration and trademark protection serve different purposes. Registering a company with the Corporate Affairs Commission does not automatically establish trademark protection for every product, service or brand associated with that company.

Businesses should assess whether important names require trademark searches and applications in the relevant jurisdictions.

## 5. Maintain an Official Logo Inventory

Businesses often accumulate several versions of a logo as their branding evolves. Without version control, outdated or unauthorised designs may continue to appear on websites, contracts, presentations and marketing materials.

Maintain a record of:

- The currently approved logo.
- Previous versions and their retirement dates.
- Editable source files and approved export formats.
- The designer or agency responsible for creation.
- Relevant design and ownership agreements.
- Applicable trademark registrations or pending applications.
- Permitted uses and brand guidelines.

Clearly identify the approved version and restrict unauthorised alterations. Keep previous versions for historical and evidential purposes without allowing them to be mistaken for current branding.

## 6. Document Brand Colours and Visual Standards

A consistent visual identity helps customers recognise a business across communication channels.

Maintain a brand guide covering:

- Approved colour specifications, including HEX, RGB and CMYK values where relevant.
- Primary and secondary typefaces.
- Logo placement and spacing.
- Approved backgrounds and contrast requirements.
- Iconography, imagery and graphic styles.
- Rules governing adaptation for print and digital media.

These specifications are primarily brand-management controls. A colour, font or graphic element is not automatically protected by intellectual property law merely because it appears in a brand guide. The applicable legal protection depends on the circumstances and relevant rights.

## 7. Inventory Marketing and Creative Materials

Marketing teams frequently create large volumes of material, including flyers, brochures, advertisements, presentations, videos and social media graphics.

For each significant item, record:

- Asset title and description.
- Creator and creation date.
- Original and editable file locations.
- Ownership or licence terms.
- Any restrictions on reproduction or modification.
- Channels where the asset is used.
- Whether the material contains third-party images, music, fonts or other content.

This is particularly important where an agency, freelancer or external photographer produced the material. Payment for a creative service should not be assumed, without reviewing the applicable agreement and law, to transfer every relevant intellectual property right.

## 8. Catalogue Website and Digital Assets

A business's online presence may contain several distinct assets requiring separate records.

These may include:

- Domain names and renewal dates.
- Website text, photographs and graphics.
- Original articles and digital publications.
- Website designs and custom components.
- Source code and software repositories.
- Databases and technical documentation.
- Relevant third-party plugins, themes and software licences.

Record who controls each asset, where the relevant files are stored and whether access depends on an employee or external provider.

Domain-name registration, copyright ownership and ownership of a website's underlying software are different matters. Each should be assessed separately.

## 9. Record Software and Technology Ownership

Software projects can involve multiple contributors, vendors, open-source components and third-party licences. A business should not assume that commissioning a developer automatically resolves every ownership or usage issue.

For each significant system, document:

- System name and purpose.
- Developers and other contributors.
- Relevant development and employment agreements.
- Source-code repository and access arrangements.
- Ownership or licensing position.
- Third-party and open-source dependencies.
- Maintenance responsibilities.
- Backup and handover procedures.

Protect confidential information and restrict access to sensitive technical materials. Where the business relies on a third-party platform, distinguish ownership of its own content and configurations from the rights granted by the platform provider.

## 10. Verify Ownership and Usage Rights

An asset's presence in the company's files does not necessarily mean the company owns all associated rights.

For each important asset, determine whether it was:

- Created by an employee within the relevant employment arrangement.
- Commissioned from a freelancer or agency.
- Acquired from another rights holder.
- Licensed for specified uses.
- Developed jointly with another party.
- Created using third-party material.

Review employment contracts, contractor agreements, assignments, licences, confidentiality agreements and relevant commercial contracts.

Where ownership is uncertain, identify the gap and obtain appropriate legal advice. An assignment or other document may be needed, depending on the circumstances, to establish or document the intended transfer of rights.

## 11. Record Registration and Protection Status

Different intellectual assets may require different forms of protection.

| Asset | Potential protection or control |
|---|---|
| Business or product brand | Trademark protection |
| Original text, photographs and videos | Copyright |
| Software and source code | Copyright, contractual controls and confidentiality measures where appropriate |
| Qualifying inventions | Patent protection |
| Qualifying product appearance | Industrial design protection |
| Confidential formulas and processes | Trade secret protection and confidentiality controls |
| Domain names | Registration and renewal management, alongside any relevant legal rights |

The appropriate approach depends on the asset and the applicable law. Some rights arise without registration, while others require registration or other formalities to obtain or maintain particular forms of protection.

For Nigerian copyright information, consult the [Nigerian Copyright Commission](https://copyright.gov.ng/) and its [Copyright e-Registration System](https://eregistration.copyright.gov.ng/ncc/). Registration can help document a work and support evidence of ownership, but it should not be confused with the creation of copyright itself.

## 12. Assess Intellectual Property Risks and Priorities

Once the register is established, evaluate which assets require attention first.

Consider:

- Commercial importance and contribution to revenue.
- Exposure to copying or unauthorised use.
- Uncertainty about ownership.
- Registration or renewal deadlines.
- Geographic markets in which protection is needed.
- Dependence on external developers or service providers.
- Potential disputes involving third-party rights.
- Costs of maintaining protection relative to business value.

Prioritise critical brands, revenue-generating content, core software and assets central to the company's competitive position.

The objective is not to register everything indiscriminately. It is to choose appropriate protection and management measures based on commercial significance and legal risk.

## 13. Establish Secure Storage and Access Controls

Intellectual assets should be stored in an approved repository rather than depending on personal email accounts or individual employees' devices.

Implement:

- Role-based access permissions.
- Secure backups and recovery procedures.
- Version control for important files.
- Clear naming and folder conventions.
- Restrictions on sharing confidential materials.
- Access reviews when staff or contractors leave.
- Documented handover procedures.

Keep ownership agreements, registration records and other supporting evidence alongside the corresponding asset records where practicable.

For sensitive information, maintain appropriate confidentiality and security controls. An inventory should identify the existence and location of confidential assets without unnecessarily exposing their contents.

## 14. Monitor Registrations, Renewals and Changes

An intellectual property inventory should be updated whenever relevant circumstances change.

Establish a review schedule covering:

- Newly created or acquired assets.
- Changes in ownership or licensing arrangements.
- Pending registration applications.
- Applicable renewal deadlines.
- New product launches and market expansions.
- Expiring third-party licences.
- Departures of employees or contractors with access to important assets.
- Suspected infringement or unauthorised use.

Assign responsibility for each follow-up action and retain evidence of completion. Review frequency should reflect the size of the portfolio, the pace of asset creation and the risks involved.

## 15. Report Portfolio Status to Management

A concise dashboard helps management understand the condition of the intellectual property portfolio.

Useful indicators include:

- Total assets recorded.
- Assets with verified ownership.
- Registered and pending rights.
- Assets requiring protection assessment.
- Upcoming renewals.
- Outstanding ownership documentation.
- High-risk third-party dependencies.
- Suspected infringement incidents.
- Corrective actions awaiting completion.

These figures should support decisions about protection, licensing, investment and risk management rather than simply measuring the number of assets recorded.

## Practical Example

A technology company believes its principal intellectual assets are its business name and logo.

A structured inventory identifies additional assets, including product names, website content, training materials, software, internal procedures and marketing designs.

The review also reveals that some graphics were created by an external agency without readily available ownership documentation, while access to a software repository remains under a former contractor's account.

Management can now prioritise ownership verification, secure access, review applicable licences and assess which brands require trademark protection.

The inventory does not automatically resolve every legal issue, but it makes previously hidden gaps visible and gives the business a practical basis for addressing them.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Assuming that intellectual property consists only of trademarks and patents.
- Failing to maintain a central asset register.
- Treating possession of a file as proof of ownership.
- Assuming that payment to a designer or developer automatically transfers all rights.
- Ignoring software, confidential information and digital content.
- Failing to distinguish owned assets from licensed third-party material.
- Keeping critical assets in personal accounts.
- Allowing outdated logos and branding to remain in circulation.
- Missing applicable registration or renewal deadlines.
- Waiting until a dispute arises before checking ownership records.

## Conclusion

Intellectual assets can support a company's reputation, competitive advantage, revenue and growth. However, their effective management begins with identifying what exists, determining who owns the relevant rights and keeping reliable records.

A structured brand and intellectual property inventory helps businesses organise their assets, uncover ownership gaps, prioritise protection and prepare for commercial transactions.

The inventory is not a substitute for registration, enforceable agreements or legal advice where required. It is the foundation that helps a business understand what it owns, what it uses and what still needs attention.

**Before protecting, commercialising or enforcing intellectual property, establish a reliable record of the assets and rights on which the business depends.**
